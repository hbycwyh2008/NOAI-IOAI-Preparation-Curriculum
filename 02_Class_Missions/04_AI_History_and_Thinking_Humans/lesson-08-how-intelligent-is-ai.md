# Session 40 — Whole-Book Synthesis: How Intelligent Is AI?

**Class duration:** 70 minutes  
**Required reading before class:** Chapter 16 and whole-book review  
**Essential question:** How should a careful thinker evaluate claims about the intelligence of an AI system?

## Required Mastery

Students must be able to:

1. Construct a timeline of symbolic AI, neural networks, machine learning, deep learning, reinforcement learning, AI springs, and AI winters.
2. Compare symbolic AI, supervised learning, deep learning, and reinforcement learning.
3. Explain the conditions supporting modern data-driven AI success.
4. Summarise strengths in large-scale pattern recognition, bounded optimisation, high-speed computation, and data processing.
5. Summarise limitations involving distribution shift, shortcut learning, common sense, causality, abstraction, transfer, and goal alignment.
6. Distinguish narrow-task performance, general intelligence, autonomy, understanding, and consciousness.
7. Evaluate optimistic and pessimistic AI claims using evidence rather than rhetoric.
8. Identify overgeneralisation from one benchmark, fluency-as-truth, prediction-as-causality, and unlimited extrapolation of progress.
9. Connect the book to task definition, data, model, loss, training, evaluation, generalisation, and limitations in Andrew Ng Machine Learning.
10. State a defensible position and identify evidence that could change it.

## Misconceptions to Reject

- One benchmark establishes general intelligence.
- Rapid progress in one area proves unlimited progress everywhere.
- One failure invalidates an entire technical approach.
- A strong personal reaction is sufficient evidence for or against machine understanding.

## AI Claim Audit

Students must answer:

1. What exact task does the system perform?
2. What are its inputs and outputs?
3. What training data or experience did it use?
4. How was success measured?
5. How do the test conditions differ from real use?
6. What are the characteristic failure modes?
7. Does the task require common sense, causality, grounding, or transfer?
8. What human choices shaped the data, objective, metric, and deployment?
9. What conclusion is supported by the evidence?
10. What larger conclusion remains unproved?

## 70-Minute Learning Cycle

| Time | Block | Required student action |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–7 | **Skill Warm-Up** | Reconstruct the whole-book timeline and match each approach to its basic learning or reasoning mechanism. |
| 7–14 | **Talk Robin 1** | Compare personal positions and identify the strongest evidence behind each. |
| 14–20 | **Entry Check** | Distinguish narrow performance, general intelligence, understanding, and consciousness. |
| 20–32 | **Core Pattern** | Apply the ten-question AI Claim Audit. |
| 32–48 | **Guided Practice** | Audit the claim: “A system achieved a high score on a difficult examination, therefore it has human-level general intelligence.” |
| 48–62 | **Independent Rebuild** | Produce a **Thinking Human’s AI Brief** containing a historical map, a capability–limitation matrix, and an evidence-based position using at least three cases from the book plus one counterargument. |
| 62–70 | **Talk Robin 2 + Evidence** | Defend the brief and state what new evidence could change the conclusion. |

## Phase Exit Evidence

The final brief must contain:

- a clear claim;
- at least three cases from the book;
- a serious counterargument;
- explicit limitations of the evidence;
- a statement of what would change the student’s mind;
- a connection to the modelling questions used in the next phase.

## Gate

The student enters Andrew Ng Machine Learning only after independently applying the AI Claim Audit to a new product or news claim and separating demonstrated performance from unsupported conclusions.
